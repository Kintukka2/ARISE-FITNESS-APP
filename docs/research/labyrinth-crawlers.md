# Labyrinth crawlers: where exploration is the game

Research for ARISE's map screen. Scope: Etrian Odyssey (series, HD Origins, Nexus), Legend of Grimrock 1–2, Wizardry (1981 and the 2024 remake), Dungeon Master and Eye of the Beholder, Ultima Underworld, Labyrinth of Touhou 2, Undernauts, Persona 3/3 Reload/5 (Tartarus, Mementos), SMT: Strange Journey, Mary Skelter, Operencia.

**Gaps, stated up front.** The session's shared web-search budget ran out after 34 searches, so I did not research Elminage, and I could not identify a game called "Labyrinth of Lost Souls". Undernauts stands in for Experience's school of crawlers. The Etrian Odyssey fan wiki (Fandom) returned HTTP 402, so several EO rules come from Let's Play mechanics write-ups and community guides instead. Each is marked. Anything marked *(unconfirmed)* comes from memory or from a single fan claim.

---

## 1. The games

### Etrian Odyssey (Atlus, 2007–2018; HD Origins Collection 2023)

**Loop.** You leave town with a guild party of five, map a stratum of themed floors on graph paper, gather materials, dodge or hunt FOEs, and go home when you run low on TP or HP. Materials sold in town unlock new gear, so each trip makes the next one go deeper.

**Signature mechanics and exact rules**
- **Drawing the map.** The touchscreen holds one sheet of graph paper per floor. Your steps fill in automatically; walls, doors and icons are drawn by you. You get a fixed set of symbols, capped per floor (Wikipedia). Director Kazuya Niinou's diary describes two ways of playing: "study style", sitting at a desk "drawing the precise map and writing memos on it", and a "relaxed style" once a floor is done. He also wrote that "the challenge and enjoyment for the player lies in planning ahead", using known enemy and treasure positions. EO2 designer Shigeo Komori: "The foundation of this game is drawing a map on the Nintendo DS' touch screen." From EO5 on, a completed floor map pays materials and unlocks a jump to that floor from town. The game scores the walls you drew against an optimal layout, so stray lines lose the reward (Retronauts).
- **FOEs** (Formido Oppugnatura Exsequens, per Atlus). These are visible mini-bosses that move one step each time you do. Most walk a fixed loop until they sense the party, then head straight for it. A second or third FOE joins a battle in progress if it reaches you before the first one falls (Wikipedia). Each combat round counts as a step, so fighting next to an FOE is dangerous (All The Tropes). EO1 shows a proximity bar of 1–3 segments for an FOE 3, 2 or 1 steps away (GameFAQs walkthrough). In EO5 some FOEs patrol fixed lines, some chase on sight, some on proximity; Siliconera: "In every situation, there is a way to avoid or escape them until you are ready." FOEs come back days after you kill them (fan claim: about 7 days, bosses 14; *unconfirmed*). Players use terrain to manage them: golem switches raise walls that pen roaming FOEs in, and some FOEs cross water, so a river is not safe ground (Retronauts).
- **Danger gauge** (EO2, from a mechanics Let's Play). The Danger Level starts at 0 on entering a floor and after each battle. Every step adds 1 plus the tile's own danger value. A Trigger Level is drawn from 16–64 inclusive, and a battle starts when the Danger Level exceeds it. The indicator changes colour at 45%, 60% and 75% of the threshold. Tiles near entrances are worth 0, and FOE zones are low. EO3 switched to "reaches or exceeds" (fan analysis). The Lure and Stalker skills raise or lower tile values.
- **Gathering** (EO3, from a community mechanics guide). Each point rolls a common period of 0–18 gathers, then a rare period of 0–4 in which every gather yields the rare item. Gathering past the rolled maximum starts a battle and ends the period; stopping early avoids it. Points refresh at in-game midnight. The Keen Nose skill adds a 1–50% chance of the rare item during the common period, and Double Crop restores 1–10 attempts. EO2's gathering ambushes were guaranteed blindsides.
- **Ways home.** The Ariadne Thread is the return-to-town consumable *(price and limits unconfirmed: the wiki was blocked)*. Each stratum starts with a geomagnetic pole that warps you to town (RPGamer, EO3). From Untold on, a Floor Jump to stairs opens once you have finished the route between them. Shortcuts: EO1 has "no through-the-wall shortcuts save for a single one just before the Final Boss, which makes repeat trips extremely tedious" (TV Tropes critique). Later games have passages that open from one side only *(a series convention I could not confirm from a primary source)*. In EO5 one backtracking shortcut opens only after a hard fight (Retronauts).
- **Drops** (EO2 rules, from a Let's Play). Up to 3 drops per monster; the third is usually conditional: kill it a certain way or within a certain time. The game checks the conditional drop first, then the rare, then the common. No rate is below 10%. Only the Scavenge skill affects drop rates, and a battle pays at most 8 drops. From EO3, Formaldehyde makes every enemy killed that turn drop everything, conditional drops included.
- **Force, Limit, Burst, Union.** These are cooldowns that run across a whole expedition. In Untold 2 and Nexus a full gauge allows a Force Boost (a 3-turn buff, after which the gauge resets). A Force Break can only be used during a Boost and "breaks" the gauge, so it cannot recharge until you return to town (ludo.guide, Nexus). Breaks need body parts, so binds can disable them.
- **Subclassing** (EO4, Nexus). A second class whose skills are capped at half their maximum level; it excludes the subclass's unique and Force skills (Fandom snippet). Some sources say it grants 5 extra skill points *(unconfirmed)*.
- **Quest board.** Requests at the tavern "range from gathering materials to bounty hunting" (Atlus, EO5). Rewards are shown up front.

**Why it works.** The map is the player's own record, and the labyrinth becomes a real place through repetition (Kill Screen). FOEs turn combat into routing: a puzzle you can read, because their patterns are fixed and they move only when you do. Vice called EO5 "incredibly difficult but honest about the danger": it is "just as good at reminding you that the threats are real... without killing you outright for not knowing", and "it's okay to run home with your pride wounded and your lives intact." The danger gauge makes random fights partly visible. A Force Break that only recharges in town ties combat to the decision to go home.

**What players criticise.** Length: Nexus's five-floor labyrinths "feel a bit grueling and elongated", and backtracking makes the game "feel a little too long at points". EO1 had no shortcuts. Untold's conveniences (auto-map, sprinting, waypoint travel, allies announcing "a secret passage nearby") "spoil everything" because "playing without drawing the lay of the land kind of kills it" (Kill Screen, which argues the original worked through "tedium and reverie"). Its limited audience: IGN said "if you gave this game to ten players you may find one or two in the group that truly enjoy it." On the HD ports, the map tools had to account for how "a player's hand blocks more of the screen than a stylus would" (Atlus HD team via GoNintendo), and reviewers found drawing on the Switch less precise than on the DS. The auto-map can be turned on or off.

### Legend of Grimrock 1 and 2 (Almost Human, 2012, 2014)

**Loop.** A four-person party moves as one block, square by square, through a real-time dungeon (LoG1: 13 levels) or across the Isle of Nex (LoG2), solving switch, plate and riddle puzzles that gate both progress and optional loot. Fights are real time, broken up by weapon and spell cooldowns.

**Signature mechanics and exact rules**
- **Secrets.** Hidden buttons in walls open stashes of "hidden (and powerful) weapons", and some levers work in combinations (RPG Codex). The stats screen tracks "your progress on the secrets" (development update). Fan guides count 71 secrets in LoG1 *(unconfirmed)*. Many of the harder puzzles are optional but pay better equipment (Wikipedia).
- **Notes.** Scrolls from Toorum, an earlier prisoner, hint at puzzles and hidden stashes. The Codex reviewer dismissed one note and got stuck on Level 9 because of it.
- **LoG2's treasure hunt.** Dig spots are marked by evidence in the world. In the cemetery, the capital letters on three epitaphs spell out a spot between two graves. A Twigroot note says to dig "between the two lanterns". A sign in the bog reads "Underneath". Notes in the Pyramid point to a chest one tile north-west of a force field. An Ancient Map shows the whole island, including places you cannot reach. Digging the relic site three times summons LoG1's final boss (GameBanshee walkthroughs).
- **Puzzle design rule** (Almost Human's LoG2 blog): "usually puzzles where the (rules) are simple but the solution is not, work the best." One puzzle, inspired by Slitherlink, asks you to step on every square of an oddly shaped room. About 40 unused ideas waited in a shared notes file.
- **The dance.** Enemies take time to turn, so the party sidesteps and stabs at their flanks. PC Gamer: "any single enemy, no matter how tough, can be dominated by a series of cowardly stab-retreats and sidesteps". Game Informer: "lazy monster design encourages the worst kind of tedious, mechanically abusive player behavior."
- **Map modes.** Old-school mode turns off the auto-map and ships a printable grid; LoG2's higher difficulties also limit saving to set points, once each.

**Why it works.** GameBanshee: "Each new area teaches you about a new element or prompts you to make one new discovery," and "players who find themselves wishing for a better axe know that they have only themselves to blame for not finding it." Choosing an island "drove the game design towards non-linearity" (Wikipedia, LoG2).

**What players criticise.** The dance, above. Puzzles that need an item found elsewhere are "a total fun killer", because players cannot tell whether they are stuck or just missing something; the reviewer wants "some better way to let the players know if they needed something more or not" (Mana Games). LoG2's open island causes "a lot of back and forth", and without quests players ask "what the heck I'm supposed to do now?" Others: arbitrary "odd twisting halls and strange dead-end passages", monsters that respawn and block the way back (Codex), timing puzzles (the weakest kind), and fixed item placement that can cripple a build (Game Developer analysis).

### Wizardry: Proving Grounds of the Mad Overlord (1981; Digital Eclipse remake, 2024)

**Loop.** Roll a party in the castle, descend ten 20×20 levels, map them on graph paper, and come back up before your casters run dry. A wipe leaves the bodies where they fell for another party to fetch.

**Rules** (Jimmy Maher, *Playing Wizardry*)
- The box came with a 20×20 graph-paper pad. The DUMAPIC spell gives coordinates from the bottom-left corner. Hazards: teleporters, spinners that turn you without telling you, wraparound edges, and dark squares you map by bumping into walls.
- Levels 1–4 hold most of the unique content; 5–9 are mostly empty (a disk-space limit). An elevator from level 4 to 9 is for "authorized users only" and needs the blue ribbon.
- You cannot save inside the maze, and a wiped party is dead at once. A new party goes down to recover the bodies. Getting lost is "almost invariably fatal". The Malor teleport can drop you inside rock, which ends the game. Later Wizardry floors are each built around one gimmick, such as spinners at every intersection (CRPG Addict).
- **Remake.** The team kept the original difficulty and offered toggles for convenience ("quality of life improvements"), even kept some fondly remembered bugs, and aimed to make "the game you remember, not the game as it actually was" (Time Extension). It has an auto-map. Justin Bailey links corpse recovery to *Demon's Souls*.

**Why it works.** Maher: the game "rewards patience, a willingness to experiment and learn from mistakes"; it "does you no favors, but simply lays out its world before you." Deciding when to turn back, with spells running low, is the constant tension: "it usually pays to err on the side of caution."

**Criticism.** It is cruel, mostly empty, and a run can be lost for good in a moment.

### Dungeon Master (FTL, 1987) and Eye of the Beholder (SSI/Westwood, 1991)

**Loop.** A real-time descent through hand-built levels with pressure plates, buttons, keys, teleports and pits. Food, water, light and stamina are carried resources.

**Rules and intent** (Maher; Time Extension)
- Level 2 teaches the parts every later puzzle is built from (keys, plates, buttons, levers, secret doors, teleports, pits), "organically and wordlessly". Pressure plates are "usually visible but occasionally hidden."
- Fair secrets: the secrets "you're least likely to find are also those least likely to be essential", and there are "usually more of those precious keys than you actually need."
- Each level has its own personality. Maher calls it "the best level design yet seen in a CRPG", and a cleared level becomes "a safe haven to return to forevermore." Torches dim gradually.
- Doug Bell: puzzles "were created by all of us"; "you needed to have fresh people as you refined the puzzles"; the team "wanted to put the player under the pressure of time."
- **Eye of the Beholder.** SSI's "Beholder Bonus" contest: one hidden feature on each of the 12 levels, confirmed by an on-screen message (Wikipedia). That these also paid experience as "special quests" is *unconfirmed*.

### Ultima Underworld (Blue Sky / Looking Glass, 1992)

The automap began as a plain record of where you had been. It then recorded "all things that you saw above a certain brightness", and gained notes written on the map, blank pages, and a clear line between explored and unexplored (Doug Church). Church on the intended play: "we hope players will explore a large area and come back for things, rather than clearing each square foot as they go."

### Labyrinth of Touhou 2 (Nise-Eikoku Shinshitsu)

**Loop.** A large party climbs a great tree floor by floor on an isometric grid; random fights are fast.

**Rules** (RPG Codex). LoT2 added wandering bosses, borrowed from EO's FOEs, that chase you if you get close. Static encounters show their level on the map. Dying sends you back to town. In LoT1 each floor had its own twist (limited visibility, confusing layouts, teleport mazes) and returning to town was free.

**Criticism.** In LoT1, with no traps and a free retreat, tension was "completely eliminated", and the reviewer called the dungeon "a bunch of misplaced effort". LoT2's dungeon was "less tedious". Player summaries call later floor gimmicks "limited and redundant".

### Undernauts: Labyrinth of Yomi (Experience, 2022)

**Rules.** The map fills in automatically, but you cannot annotate it. Symbols on squares mark encounters, with occasional random battles on top. Yomi flowers create doors, ladders, bridges and fast-travel points. A wipe wakes you at base camp, and reviving costs money. Saving is automatic only.

**Why it works.** Symbol encounters "let you explore freely and fight at your own pace." Expect to "explore every space edge to edge."

**Criticism.** With no notes or explored-area markers, hunting for hidden passages is tedious. Areas feel "somewhat bare", movement is clunky, and difficulty spikes force grinding (RPGFan, Siliconera).

### Persona 3 / 3 Reload (Tartarus) and Persona 5 Royal (Mementos)

**Loop.** A tower of procedurally generated floors climbed in short visits, broken into blocks by story bosses; the party heals at the entrance. Mementos is the same idea as a subway.

**Rules**
- **Original P3 fatigue.** Long Tartarus visits made characters Tired or Sick. Reload removed it: per a 4Gamer interview, it "does not mesh well" with the calendar, getting Sick was random, and "since the Fatigue system actively affected how much fun players had, Atlus chose to remove it" (Siliconera).
- **The Reaper.** From floor 62 in Reload, lingering on one floor for 3–5 real-time minutes summons a super-boss, announced by red screen edges and rattling chains. The timer pauses in battles and menus but does not reset (Game8, Prima). In Mementos the limit is about 2 minutes 30 seconds, and it resets when you change floors or clear every Shadow (fan guide). Leaving by stairs or teleporter escapes it.
- **Reload's additions.** Breakable objects drop Twilight Fragments, which open rare chests and heal the party at clocks. Monad Doors are optional, harder rooms with rare chests, and finishing one "reveals the full map of its floor and the next floor" (Push Square).
- **Shuffle Time.** After a battle; guaranteed by finishing with an All-Out Attack. Pick one card. Wand cards multiply experience from 1.1× (rank 1) to 2.5× (King). Major Arcana effects last "until you leave Tartarus", a visit allows only a few of them, and a full set triggers Arcana Burst, raising every Minor Arcana rank by one (ludo.guide).
- **Mementos in Royal.** Flowers (common, they respawn) buy SP items from Jose. Stamps (rarer, found at floor ends) buy permanent experience and money bonuses of up to +100% each. The music changes with depth (Goomba Stomp).

**Criticism.** Tartarus floors are "differently colored randomized corridors" and "a giant, procedurally generated tower of samey-looking floors". Critics argue that "even the mildest form" of interactivity, such as Persona 4's light puzzles, would have helped. Reload's stated fix was to "increase the density of game elements and scenery" (Takuya Yamaguchi). Mementos was "boring, tedious, and annoyingly necessary". Royal's stamps helped some players, while others found collecting them "braindead boredom" (Goomba Stomp and other reviews).

### SMT: Strange Journey / Redux (Atlus, 2009 / 2017)

Grid sectors full of damage floors, warps and puzzles. Demonica apps open areas that "would be completely blocked off": some "unlock doors or deactivate traps", others "shift the layout of an entire area". Forma is a material picked up in the field or dropped by demons, and it unlocks gear. The field quicksave was praised as "very handheld-friendly". Criticism: Eridanus is "much more frustrating than others", and Redux's added dungeon, the Womb of Grief, with its warp puzzles and invisible floors, felt "a chore" (RPGFan).

### Mary Skelter: Nightmares (Compile Heart, 2017)

Each dungeon has an invincible Nightmare that stalks you in real time. When one appears, the screen darkens and the minimap disappears (except on Easy), and a gauge shows your distance from it. Destroying one of its parts knocks it down, which buys time to get past. Destroying the dungeon's core lowers its defence, so it can be beaten at the next meeting. Nightmares can strike during other battles (Tech-Gaming). Reviewers found the chases "hauntingly memorable" and said they make you "more cautious and thorough". The complaint: a Nightmare encounter can stop you saving, healing or leaving.

### Operencia: The Stolen Sun (Zen Studios, 2019)

A linear grid crawler. Each map's optional puzzles, false walls and hidden passages are counted, and "the player can see their progress towards finding all these secrets on the map screen." Puzzles are "usually requiring operating mechanisms in some proper order". Complaints: companions give away solutions, and exploration choices are limited (RPG Site).

## 2. Cross-cutting principles

1. **Moving has to cost something, or the map is a menu.** Every game here charges for steps: EO's danger gauge and FOE steps, the Reaper's timer, Wizardry's dwindling spells, Dungeon Master's food and light. The one that didn't, Labyrinth of Touhou 1 with its free retreat and no traps, had its tension "completely eliminated". Shortcuts, threads, poles and floor jumps are valuable only because walking costs.
2. **Visible threat makes the map a puzzle.** EO's FOEs, LoT2's chasing bosses, Undernauts's symbols and Mary Skelter's Nightmares all show the danger, and the player routes around it. They are well liked when their movement is fixed and readable ("a wise and observant player can understand and exploit their actions") and when there is always a way out (EO5). Players turn on them when they block saving, healing or leaving (Mary Skelter), when they are timed in real time (the Reaper), or when they can be cheesed (Grimrock's dance).
3. **Honest warning beats hidden odds.** EO's coloured danger gauge, LoT2's encounter levels and Persona 3 Reload's revealed Shuffle Time cards all tell the player what is coming. Game Developer's taxonomy calls hidden spike tiles unfair because they are "fairly indistinguishable from truly random encounters." Players prefer encounters they choose (Undernauts: "fight at your own pace"), and Persona 3 Reload dropped random sickness because it was random.
4. **Going home is the real decision.** Loot at risk, a resource that drains, the walk back, and a few relief points you choose (Ariadne Thread, a pole, a shortcut). Nexus's Force Break, refilled only in town, puts that decision into combat too.
5. **Fair secrets are optional, counted and signposted locally.** Dungeon Master: the hardest to find are the least essential. Operencia and Grimrock count them. Grimrock marks them with clues in the world (epitaphs, lanterns, notes). The rule should be simple and the solution not (Almost Human). Never hide a must-have item somewhere else without saying so (Mana Games).
6. **Each floor needs its own identity, or depth is just a number.** Wizardry IV and LoT1 give each floor one gimmick; Dungeon Master gives each level a personality; Strange Journey's apps change layouts; Mementos changes its music with depth. Tartarus is the counter-example everyone cites, and Reload's fix was density and landmarks.
7. **The map is something the player owns.** EO's map is drawn by the player, Ultima Underworld's takes notes, EO5 pays for a finished map. Undernauts was criticised for allowing no notes. Convenience fights with reverie (Kill Screen on Untold), and on a phone, full hand-drawing loses: the player's hand covers the screen (Atlus HD team).
8. **Conditions turn fights into puzzles.** EO's conditional drops, Persona's All-Out finish that guarantees Shuffle Time, and EO's binds that disable Force Breaks all reward how a fight is won, not only whether.

## 3. Ideas for ARISE, ranked

First, the gap. In ARISE, walking back through cleared rooms is free and takes no time (`raid.moveTo`), and withdrawing is free once you reach the entrance. So nothing that relieves the walk home (shortcuts, an Ariadne Thread, waystones) has value today, and once a room is cleared the plan is a menu, which is the Labyrinth of Touhou 1 problem. The only tension left on the plan is health worn down by side-room fights. Ideas 2 and 6 put a deterministic cost on movement; most of the rest work without it.

**What ARISE already does as well as or better than these games:**
- The **brief** (24 simulated runs of the real engine) and **foe intents that are promises** are more honest than anything here. EO's FOEs are readable only after you study them.
- **No random encounters at all.** Every fight is a room you choose to enter, which is what players prefer (Undernauts, Operencia).
- The **haul banked and lost on a wipe** is Wizardry's and EO's return-home tension, already settled in one call.
- **Shrines** are Persona 3 Reload's run-long Major Arcana with the choice shown, which is better than the original's blind draw.
- The **Gatekeeper and its vault** are Monad Doors: an optional harder room with a richer reward, marked as locked on the plan.
- **Hidden passages** that open when you clear a room beside them follow Dungeon Master's fairness rule: optional, never essential.
- **Rests found and kept for later** are EO's springs and Reload's clocks done better.
- **Fatigue is deterministic and visible** (about 5 an hour), which avoids the random sickness that got Persona 3's fatigue removed.
- The **fog remembers what you saw** while the map stands (`s.gates.seen`), which is EO's automap without the stylus.

**One risk to fix.** The Gate Survey costs a flat 60 shards with no per-run cap, while the campaign's hunters end with 16,800–79,900 unspent shards. At the top, the fog becomes optional. This is Untold's "convenience spoils the place" in economic form. Price the Survey by grade, or allow one per run.

### 1. Show the threat on the plan — from EO's FOEs, LoT2's encounter levels and Undernauts's symbols. Cost: small to medium.
- **What to build.** A seen fight room shows its pack: family glyph and count, plus a ring for a Gatekeeper-strength creature. Tapping a room on the frontier (`raid.options`) runs the room's fight on the auto policy from the party's current state, with the room's own seed. Because fights are deterministic, the result is exact, not an estimate: `Auto: cleared, party at 58%` or `Auto: falls`.
- **Plugs into.** `map.chamberOf` and `raid.chamberAt`, `ui/map.tsx`, and the projection code.
- **Decision added.** Which branch to clear with the health you have left, and which room to leave for after a rest. Fights become routing decisions, the way FOEs make them.
- **Constraints.** The preview only reads the engine and is exactly what Auto would do; playing by hand can beat it. The fog still hides rooms not yet seen. Nothing to recalibrate.
- **Better than the sources:** EO only colour-codes its FOEs; this gives the actual outcome.

### 2. Wanderers: creatures that move when you move — from EO's FOEs and LoT2's chasing bosses. Cost: large.
- **What to build.** At C-Rank and above, each floor holds one Wanderer (two at A and S), drawn from the map seed: a named creature of the Gate's family with an escort, at Gatekeeper strength. It walks a fixed loop of side rooms, its beat.
  - **Clock.** It moves one room each time the hunter enters a room or walks back to one (`raid.choose`, `raid.moveTo`), and on a new Hold action (stay put for one tick). Each tick is written to the run, so its position is a pure function of the map seed and the run's moves.
  - **Showing it.** In a seen room it is drawn with an arrow to the room it enters next. Its next room is a promise, just like a foe's intent. Within one corridor of the hunter, its next move turns toward the hunter, and the arrow shows that first. Fogged rooms within two corridors of it show footprints (EO1's proximity bars).
  - **Meeting.** If the hunter enters its room, or it enters the hunter's, the fight starts there. Fight rounds do not tick, which avoids EO's pile-ups.
  - **Pay.** It pays like a Gatekeeper, stays around at better extraction odds (a named Shadow), and carries a condition drop (idea 3).
- **Simulation.** Its beat never includes a room on the System's road, the same rule that keeps the Gatekeeper off it. The brief, Auto and calibration therefore never meet it, and the Gate tunings don't move; test that, as `tests/map.test.ts` tests the System's choices. Leaving the road becomes a real choice, which the "walk off the marked road" Daily Quest already rewards.
- **Decision and feeling added.** Timing and routing ("planning ahead", Niinou), danger shown honestly (Vice), and a reason for shortcuts (idea 6) and a Hold button. Walking back now costs ticks.
- **Avoiding the sources' failures.** It never blocks the entrance, rests or stairs, it never chases outside its beat (no Mary Skelter lock-ins), and nothing is real time.
- **New daily-quest kinds:** "Fell a Wanderer" and "Clear a floor without meeting its Wanderer".

### 3. How it fell: condition drops — from EO's conditional drops. Cost: small to medium.
- **What to build.** Each creature family and each boss gets one stated condition, for example: fell it while Stunned; with the hunter's own blow; within 3 rounds; while its intent targets a Shadow; with a skill of a given Path. Meeting it adds a stated bonus to the haul: double the family's material, the boss's trophy, or a blueprint chance.
- **Where it shows.** On the creature's card, the Gate board and the fight's head (`Condition: met`). Nothing hidden, in line with "every reward stated up front".
- **Constraints.** Checked from the fight's event log. Banked on the run and paid in `raid.finish`.
- **Why it helps ARISE's own weaknesses.** Auto never aims for conditions, so playing by hand pays something Auto doesn't, which targets "Auto can carry most play" and "early turns are shallow". Clear odds don't change, so no recalibration. The campaign plays Auto and earns no condition drops, so its economy stays where it is.

### 4. Veins: gathering with stated odds — from EO3's gathering (common period, rare period, an ambush past the limit). Cost: small to medium.
- **What to build.** A new side-room kind, `vein`, holding ore, hide or cores by the Gate's family. It gives 2 sure harvests, then each further harvest has a stated chance (say 1 in 4) to wake a pack that acts first. One vein per room, harvested only between steps. A vein in a cleared room can be worked later, which gives walking back a purpose. A vein yielding a material the hunter tracks wears the Forge's ember.
- **Plugs into.** The haul, `data/forge.ts` materials, `raid.resolve`.
- **Constraints.** Every harvest is seeded from the run, the node and the attempt number. The System takes the sure harvests only and never risks one, so the projection plays it, and veins sit off the road anyway.
- **Feeling.** Push your luck with the odds shown, feeding the Forge.
- Rerun the campaign, since it changes material income.

### 5. Floors with an identity: traits, landmarks and music by depth — from Wizardry IV and LoT1's one-gimmick floors, Dungeon Master's level personalities, Tartarus as the warning and Mementos Royal's music. Cost: medium.
- **Traits.** Each floor of a C+ Gate draws one of about eight traits, shown under `Floor 2/3`. Each is one rule the System can play:
  - Flooded: no rests.
  - Ossuary: creatures linger more often.
  - Echoing: shrines offer three blessings.
  - Rich: two veins.
  - Warded: a passage needs both ends cleared.
  - Hunted: a second Wanderer.
  - Still: no traps.
  - Dark: a room is seen only from a cleared room beside it by corridor, never through a passage.
- **Landmarks.** One per floor: a named room (an Association camp, a fallen statue, a dry well) that always shows through the fog, like the boss's room. It gives the player a fixed point to steer by, holds a unique event, and anchors the notes in idea 7.
- **Music.** The WebAudio score shifts with each floor of depth.
- **Constraints.** Drawn from the map seed. Traits that touch the road change the odds, the brief plays them, and the gates and the Spire must be recalibrated.
- **Why.** An S-Rank Gate is 96 rooms, and Nexus shows what five samey floors feel like.

### 6. What a run opens stays open today: one-sided shortcuts and waystones — from EO's shortcuts, Floor Jump and geomagnetic poles, and Wizardry's elevator. Cost: medium to large.
- **Shortcuts.** On floors 2 and below, a sealed door joins a deep room to a room near the entrance. It opens only from the deep side, and once opened it is remembered with the map, like `seen` (for example `s.gates.opened`).
- **Waystones.** Stairs down, once reached, become a waystone.
- **Re-entry.** A hunter who withdraws (to heal, to bank the haul, to let fatigue fade) and re-enters the same Gate the same day may start at the deepest waystone or use the shortcut. Rooms skipped pay nothing, since `paceOf` already shares pay out by road length. Every entry still adds fatigue, which is the cost.
- **Effect.** A 3–4 floor Gate becomes a two-trip expedition, EO's core loop, without selling time.
- **Simulation.** `map.systemRoad` runs over the graph plus any opened edges, so the brief stays exact for that hunter.
- **Checks.** The campaign must confirm that clears per unit of fatigue don't jump. With idea 2, shortcuts also go around beats.

### 7. Notes and buried caches — from Grimrock's Toorum notes and LoG2's epitaph and lantern clues. Cost: small to medium.
- **What to build.** A cache room sometimes also holds a hunter's note pointing at a hidden cache elsewhere on the map ("Past the dry well, the second room on the left; the wall gives"). Clearing the named room reveals it. Clues are templates over landmarks and corridor counts, all drawn from the seed and checked against `docs/VOICE.md`. The writers are Association hunters, so the System is never mentioned.
- **Constraints.** Off the road, with no change to the odds. The cache is banked and paid in `raid.finish`.
- **Feeling.** Deduction ("only themselves to blame for not finding it") without a must-have item hidden elsewhere.

### 8. Count the secrets and pay for a charted floor — from Operencia's secret counter, Grimrock's stats screen and EO5's map-completion reward. Cost: small.
- **What to build.** The plan's head shows `Secrets 1/3` (passages, vault, buried caches) and `Charted 17/24` for each floor. A floor seen in full by walking (not by Survey) adds a small stated material bounty to the haul.
- **Fits with.** Every Room and Cartographer stay secret; the counter never names them. It gives exploring off the road a visible goal.

### 9. Pins on the plan — from EO's map icons, Ultima Underworld's notes and Undernauts's missing notes. Cost: small.
- **What to build.** Long-press a room to place one of four marks: avoid, return, vein, note. They are kept with the map's `seen` memory until midnight.
- **Interface.** Reuses the bag's wheel, one-handed, with no drawing (the HD team's warning about the hand covering the screen).
- Matters most once ideas 2, 4 and 7 exist.

### 10. Surge: one big blow per Gate — from Nexus's Force Break. Cost: medium.
- **What to build.** The hunter's Surge gauge fills over the fights of a run. When full, the hunter may unleash their highest Path's Surge. After that it stays broken until the hunter leaves the Gate.
- **Decision added.** Spend it on the Gatekeeper and its vault, or keep it for the boss? It ties combat to the run's arc and adds an early-game decision.
- **Constraints.** Deterministic. Auto's rule: Surge on the boss's first round. The brief plays that, so gates, the Spire and Ascension need recalibrating.

### 11. Ascension past level ten: map rules, not just bigger numbers — from Wizardry's dark zones and LoG2's hard modes, which take away the map and limit saves. Cost: medium.
- **What to build.** Every few levels past ten add a map rule instead of only more strength: Dark Gates (the fog forgets what it saw), no Survey, Wanderers awake (beats may cross the road, so the projection now meets them), rests unseen.
- **Plugs into.** The ladder keeps teaching instead of only inflating numbers. Recalibrate the levels against `keptUpHunter` as now.
- **Addresses** "an invested hunter outgrows the difficulty".

### 12. Go back for it: recovering the haul — from Wizardry's corpse recovery and Demon's Souls. Cost: medium to large; build last.
- **What to build.** When the hunter is carried out, the run's haul stays in the room where they fell, marked on that Gate's plan. The Gate stays pinned to the board until the next midnight redraw after the lockout lifts. A later run that reaches the room recovers the haul, paid in that run's `raid.finish`.
- **Pillar 5 holds.** A wipe still loses everything at the time; recovery is a new run with its own risk.
- **Risks.** The board's redraw and the 12-hour lockout make this the most intrusive idea, and the economy must be rerun.

**Do not borrow:**
- Hand-drawn maps: phones, daily maps, and the hand over the screen.
- Real-time timers or chases (the Reaper, Nightmares).
- Random sickness.
- Random or hidden-tile encounters.
- Puzzles that need an item found elsewhere.
- Timing puzzles.
